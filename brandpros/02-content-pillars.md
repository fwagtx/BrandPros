# 02 — Content Pillars (Recurring Series)

Six series, each with **its own format rules**. Different structures, openers and visual treatments are what make the channel look like a *show* and not like templated, mass-produced content. That matters for YouTube's inauthentic/repetitious content policy.

Target mix for the first 90 days (by number of Shorts):

| Series | Share | Primary job |
|---|---|---|
| The $1B Mistake | 20% | Reach (strongest hooks) |
| Dead Brands | 20% | Facebook nostalgia plus comments |
| How They Make Money | 20% | Long-form RPM plus sponsor-friendly content |
| The Comeback | 15% | Shares (feel-good) |
| Founder Files | 15% | Watch time and a human angle |
| Brand Wars | 10% | Comments and debate |

---

## 1. The $1B Mistake 🟥
**What:** The single decision that cost a company a fortune, and whether it was *really* a mistake at the time.

**Format rules:**
- Short: opens on the dollar figure on screen ("$50,000,000") and a question ("Would you have said no?").
- Structure: **The offer → Why saying no made sense then → What happened next → The real mistake** (often not the famous one).
- Always ends with the **"Pros know the lesson"** beat: one sentence of transferable business wisdom.
- Visual signature: a giant red price tag that "marks down" as the story progresses.

**Examples:** Blockbuster passing on Netflix, Kodak's 1975 digital camera, Quaker buying Snapple, Yahoo turning down Microsoft.

**Why it works:** Loss aversion and hindsight are irresistible ("I would never…"). Huge numbers make a thumb-stopping hook. It also produces debate in the comments ("actually it was the right call"), which drives engagement. The "was it really a mistake?" angle adds a POV that recap channels lack.

## 2. Dead Brands 🟨 (sepia)
**What:** Stores, products and companies people grew up with and what actually happened to them. It's the Facebook engine.

**Format rules:**
- Opens with a **nostalgia trigger** in the first two seconds: a cartoon of the storefront or product and "Remember this?"
- Structure: **The glory days (with one sensory detail: the smell, the jingle, the catalog) → The crack → The collapse → Where it is now** (many "dead" brands live on as a name, a website, or a surprising successor).
- Visual signature: sepia filter on the "glory days" section, switching to full color for "where it is now".
- Every Facebook post asks a memory question ("What did you buy there?").

**Examples:** Toys "R" Us, Sears, Blockbuster, Woolworth (now Foot Locker's corporate ancestor), Pan Am, Circuit City, RadioShack.

**Why it works:** Nostalgia is one of the most-shared emotions, especially for the 35+ Facebook audience. Memory prompts produce long comment threads, which Facebook's ranking rewards. "Where it is now" gives a payoff twist.

---

## 3. How They Make Money 🟨 (gold)
**What:** The business model hiding in plain sight. Usually the product you think of isn't the money-maker.

**Format rules:**
- Opens with a **myth vs. reality** line ("Costco isn't really in the retail business.").
- Structure: **What you think they sell → What actually pays the bills (one clear chart) → Why it's genius → What it means for you as a customer.** The last beat is the financeprostv bridge.
- Visual signature: an animated "money flow" diagram (cartoon cash moving from customer → company) and one simple bar or pie chart in brand colors.
- Only use figures from the company's own filings (10-K, annual report), always with the fiscal year stated.

**Examples:** Costco memberships, McDonald's real estate, Starbucks' stored-value cards, airline loyalty programs, Rolex being owned by a foundation.

**Why it works:** It has the highest RPM potential (business and finance advertisers). It's evergreen, easy to sponsor (investing apps, newsletters, business tools), and satisfies curiosity with a clean "aha".

---

## 4. The Comeback 🟩
**What:** Brands that were on the edge of death and came back.

**Format rules:**
- Opens at **the lowest point** ("In 2003, LEGO was losing money on almost every set it sold [VERIFY]").
- Structure: **Rock bottom → Who stepped in → The unpopular decision → The turnaround in numbers → Today.**
- Visual signature: the red falling arrow bends into a green rising arrow at the turnaround moment.
- Tone: uplifting, but always name the *specific* decision that turned it around (not "they worked hard").

**Examples:** LEGO (2004), Apple (1997), Marvel (bankrupt in 1996, sold to Disney for $4B in 2009), Domino's "our pizza sucks" campaign, Ford mortgaging its blue-oval logo in 2006.

**Why it works:** Feel-good content gets shared, and it provides a positive counterweight to Mistakes and Dead Brands so the channel isn't all doom. It's also great for sponsors that want positive brand adjacency.

## 5. Founder Files 🟦 (navy and gold)
**What:** The person behind the logo, focused on their failures *before* success and at least one little-known fact.

**Format rules:**
- Opens with a **"before they were famous"** contrast ("Before KFC, Harland Sanders was fired from more jobs than you've had [VERIFY framing]").
- Structure: **Failures → The pivot moment → The deal that made (or broke) them → Myth check (what the internet gets wrong).**
- Every episode includes a **Myth Check** segment. It's a signature beat that reinforces the brand's credibility.
- Visual signature: a caricature of the founder in a gold "file folder" frame, with era-accurate cartoon settings.
- **Mostly deceased or historical founders.** Living founders only for well-documented, neutral or positive stories.

**Examples:** Colonel Sanders, Walt Disney losing Oswald the Lucky Rabbit, Henry Ford's company that became Cadillac, Ruth Handler (Barbie), Madam C.J. Walker.

**Why it works:** People connect with people. Failure-to-success arcs are the most reliable story structure. It also overlaps naturally with historypros.

---

## 6. Brand Wars 🟥🟦 (split)
**What:** Rivalries between companies or people that shaped what we buy.

**Format rules:**
- Opens with a **split-screen face-off** of two caricatures or products and "This rivalry split a town in half."
- Structure: **Round 1 (origin) → Round 2 (escalation) → Knockout moment → Who won (with numbers) → Ask the viewer to pick a side.**
- Visual signature: a boxing-match graphic with round cards and a scoreboard.
- The comment prompt is always "Team ___ or Team ___?"

**Examples:** Adidas vs. Puma (the Dassler brothers), Coke vs. Pepsi (New Coke), Nintendo vs. Sega, Ford vs. Ferrari at Le Mans, Duracell vs. Energizer bunnies.

**Why it works:** Picking sides triggers comments, stitches and duets. The boxing format is instantly recognizable and very different from the other five.

## Series-wide rules (originality checklist)

Every video must have:
- [ ] A **thesis sentence** in the script ("The real mistake was ___").
- [ ] At least **one fact most viewers don't know**, sourced.
- [ ] **Original illustration**, meaning scenes generated or drawn for this video in the house style (no raw reposted clips from other creators or TV).
- [ ] A **series-specific structure** (above), with no two consecutive Shorts from the same series on the same day.
- [ ] Human review of the script's POV and facts before production (see `08-production-workflow.md`).

## Occasional formats (use once the six pillars are running)
- **"Name Game"**: the origin of brand names (Häagen-Dazs is made-up Danish-sounding Bronx ice cream; Nike is the Greek goddess of victory). Quick 20–30s filler for busy weeks.
- **"Logo Lore"**: why logos look the way they do.
- **"Price Then vs. Now"**: nostalgia price comparisons (FB favorite; always inflation-adjust using the BLS CPI calculator).
- **Viewer Verdict**: a community post or poll where the audience picks next week's Dead Brand.
